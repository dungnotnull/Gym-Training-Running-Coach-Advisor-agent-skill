## Progressive Overload

### Paper 1: Kraemer, W. J., & Ratamess, N. A. (2004). Fundamentals of Resistance Training: Progression and Exercise Prescription. *Strength and Conditioning Journal*, 28(8), 60-67.

**Key Findings:**
- Progressive overload requires systematic increases in training stress over time
- Three primary variables: intensity, volume, and frequency
- Novices progress faster than trained athletes
- Training age affects recovery needs

**Practical Applications:**
- Increase load by 2-10% when target reps achieved
- Deload every 4-6 weeks for advanced lifters
- Volume increases precede intensity increases

### Paper 2: Rhea, M. R., et al. (2003). A Meta-Analysis to Determine the Dose Response for Strength Development. *Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise*, 35(2), 454.

**Key Findings:**
- 3 sets per exercise superior to 1 or 2 sets for strength gains
- 70-85% 1RM optimal for strength development
- Training frequency of 3 days per week sufficient for novice to intermediate
- Advanced athletes may require higher frequency (4-6 days)

**Practical Applications:**
- Default to 3 sets per exercise
- Target 75% 1RM for working sets
- Progress to higher frequency with experience

### Paper 3: Campos, G. E., et al. (2002). Muscular Adaptations to Short-Term Resistance Training in Powerlifters. *Journal of Strength and Conditioning Research*, 16(2), 255.

**Key Findings:**
- Hypertrophy occurs at 60-80% 1RM
- Maximum strength requires 85-95% 1RM
- Power development requires 30-45% 1RM
- Volume (sets × reps) is critical driver of adaptation

**Practical Applications:**
- Phase training: hypertrophy → strength → power
- Intensity must match training goal
- Volume reduction when intensity increases

### Paper 4: Haff, G. G., & Triplett, N. T. (2015). Essentials of Strength Training and Conditioning. *Human Kinetics*.

**Key Findings:**
- 40-60% 1RM for power endurance
- 85-100% 1RM for maximal strength
- Rest intervals: 1-2 min for hypertrophy, 3-5 min for strength
- Training frequency depends on muscle group recovery

**Practical Applications:**
- Match rest intervals to intensity
- Split training allows higher frequency
- Recovery time between sessions critical

## Periodization Models

### Paper 5: Bompa, T. O., & Buzzichelli, C. (2018). *Periodization: Theory and Methodology of Training* (6th ed.). Human Kinetics.

**Key Findings:**
- Linear periodization: high volume → low volume, low intensity → high intensity
- Undulating periodization: weekly variation in intensity
- Block periodization: concentrated training blocks
- Recovery periods essential between phases

**Practical Applications:**
- Beginners: Linear periodization
- Intermediate: Undulating (DUP)
- Advanced: Block periodization
- Always include deload weeks

### Paper 6: Stone, M. H., et al. (2000). Strength and Conditioning: Terminology and Overtraining. *Strength and Conditioning Journal*, 24(4), 45-56.

**Key Findings:**
- Periodization prevents overtraining
- Tapering improves performance by 2-4%
- Peaking requires 7-14 days
- Fitness cannot be maintained indefinitely

**Practical Applications:**
- Plan tapering before competitions
- Peaking is short-term (2-4 weeks)
- Return to base building after peak

### Paper 7: Fleck, S. J., & Kraemer, W. J. (2004). *Designing Resistance Training Programs* (3rd ed.). Human Kinetics.

**Key Findings:**
- Traditional periodization vs. Daily Undulating Periodization (DUP)
- DUP produces superior strength gains
- Variation prevents adaptation plateau
- Recovery requirements increase with training age

**Practical Applications:**
- Heavy/Medium/Light weekly pattern
- Rotate exercises to prevent staleness
- Monitor fatigue and adjust accordingly

### Paper 8: Issurin, V. B. (2008). Block Periodization vs. Traditional Training. *Strength and Conditioning Journal*, 32(9), 21-22.

**Key Findings:**
- Block periodization superior for advanced athletes
- Accumulation → Transmutation → Realization blocks
- Each block 4-6 weeks
- Specialization increases over time

**Practical Applications:**
- 4-week accumulation block (high volume)
- 4-week transmutation block (high intensity)
- 4-week realization block (competition-specific)
- 1-week deload between blocks

## Running Training & VDOT

### Paper 9: Daniels, J. (2013). *Daniels' Running Formula* (3rd ed.). Human Kinetics.

**Key Findings:**
- VDOT accurately predicts race performance
- Training zones based on VDOT scores
- Zone 1: Easy/recovery (59-74% VO2max)
- Zone 2: Marathon pace (75-82% VO2max)
- Zone 3: Threshold/tempo (83-88% VO2max)
- Zone 4: Interval/VO2max (89-94% VO2max)
- Zone 5: Repetition/speed (95-100% VO2max)

**Practical Applications:**
- Calculate training zones from recent race times
- 80% training in zones 1-2, 20% in zones 4-5
- Minimal zone 3 training ("gray zone")
- Taper 7-14 days before races

### Paper 10: Seiler, S., & Tønnessen, E. (2015). Intervals, Thresholds, and Long Slow Distance: The Role of Intensity and Duration in Endurance Training. *Frontiers in Physiology*, 6, 485.

**Key Findings:**
- Polarized training: 80% low intensity, 20% high intensity
- Pyramidal training: moderate distribution across all zones
- Elite endurance athletes use polarized distribution
- Zone 3 (threshold) training less effective than believed

**Practical Applications:**
- Default to polarized 80/20 distribution
- Limit zone 3 training
- Most running should be easy (conversational pace)
- Quality sessions: zone 4 or zone 5

### Paper 11: Laursen, P. B., & Jenkins, D. G. (2002). The Scientific Basis for High-Intensity Interval Training. *Sports Medicine*, 34(6), 27-29.

**Key Findings:**
- HIIT improves VO2max more than moderate training
- Interval format: 4-6 × 4-6 min at 90-100% VO2max
- Recovery intervals: 1-2 min for each interval
- 2-3 sessions per week sufficient

**Practical Applications:**
- One quality interval session per week
- Build aerobic base first with zone 2 running
- Add zone 4 intervals after base established
- Recovery between quality sessions critical

### Paper 12: Foster, C., et al. (2006). A New Approach to Tempo-Running. *Track Coach*, 183, 34-38.

**Key Findings:**
- Tempo runs improve lactate threshold
- Sustained effort at "comfortably hard" pace
- Duration: 20-40 minutes continuous
- Progress by extending duration, not increasing pace

**Practical Applications:**
- One tempo run per week during build phase
- Start with 20 min, extend to 40 min
- Pace: "hard but sustainable"
- Critical for marathon preparation

### Paper 13: Billat, L. V., et al. (2001). Intermittent Running at VO2max and Intermittent Training at 90% VO2max. *Journal of Sports Sciences*, 19(9), 801.

**Key Findings:**
- 30-30 training: 30 sec at 100% VO2max, 30 sec recovery
- Improves VO2max and running economy
- 5-8 intervals per session
- 2 sessions per week maximum

**Practical Applications:**
- 30-30 intervals for VO2max improvement
- 5-8 intervals sufficient
- Extend recovery if intervals decline
- Use in peaking phase for 5K/10K

## Recovery Monitoring

### Paper 14: Kreher, J. B., & Schwartz, J. B. (2012). Overtraining Syndrome: A Practical Guide. *Sports Health*, 4(4), 241-250.

**Key Findings:**
- Overtraining syndrome (OTS) distinct from normal fatigue
- Warning signs: persistent fatigue, performance decline, sleep disturbances
- Mood changes: irritability, depression, lack of motivation
- Recovery from OTS requires weeks to months

**Practical Applications:**
- Monitor sleep quality, mood, and performance trends
- Resting heart rate elevation >5 bpm indicates poor recovery
- Deload immediately when multiple warning signs present
- Prevention through planned recovery weeks

### Paper 15: Sluka, K. A., et al. (2017). Sleep, Recovery, and Performance in Athletes. *European Journal of Sport Science*, 17(sup1), S198.

**Key Findings:**
- Athletes require 8-10 hours sleep per night
- Sleep deprivation impairs recovery and performance
- Sleep quality as important as duration
- Naps can supplement night sleep

**Practical Applications:**
- Prioritize sleep during heavy training blocks
- Consistent sleep schedule (±30 min)
- Dark, cool bedroom (65-68°F)
- 20-30 min naps beneficial

### Paper 16: Nederhof, E., et al. (2018). Monitoring Athletes' Readiness: What Do We Miss? *Frontiers in Physiology*, 9, 244.

**Key Findings:**
- Subjective markers: sleep quality, mood, muscle soreness, motivation
- Objective markers: resting heart rate, HRV, performance trends
- Acute:Chronic workload ratio predicts injury risk
- Ratio >1.5 indicates high risk

**Practical Applications:**
- Daily subjective monitoring recommended
- HRV useful if consistent measurement protocol
- Calculate training stress: 7-day acute vs 28-day chronic
- Be conservative when ratio exceeds 1.3

### Paper 17: Meeusen, R., & Duclos, M. (2004). Use of Acute:Chronic Workload Ratio. *Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise*, 36(4), S17.

**Key Findings:**
- Acute workload: past 7 days training load
- Chronic workload: past 28 days average
- Ratio >1.5 predicts 70% injury probability
- "Sweet spot": 0.8-1.3 for optimal adaptation

**Practical Applications:**
- Track weekly training volume
- Calculate 4-week rolling average
- Adjust training when ratio exceeds 1.3
- Use ratio to guide deload timing

## Overtraining Syndrome

### Paper 18: Meeusen, R., et al. (2006). Inflammation and Overtraining Syndrome. *Sports Medicine*, 36(3), 251-258.

**Key Findings:**
- OTS involves inflammatory response dysregulation
- Cytokine markers elevated in overtrained athletes
- Hormonal disturbances: decreased testosterone, increased cortisol
- Immune system suppression common

**Practical Applications:**
- Medical assessment if OTS suspected
- Blood work: hormone panels, inflammatory markers
- Significant rest required for recovery (4-8 weeks minimum)
- Prevention through proper periodization

### Paper 19: Cadsee, J., & LaRoche, D. P. (2009). Overreaching and Overtraining. *Strength and Conditioning Journal*, 31(1), 35-39.

**Key Findings:**
- Functional overreaching: beneficial, requires 3-5 days recovery
- Non-functional overreaching: detrimental, requires weeks
- Distinguish between normal fatigue and overtraining
- Performance decline is key indicator

**Practical Applications:**
- Planned functional overreaching in training blocks
- Deload before non-functional overreaching occurs
- Monitor performance trends closely
- Reduce training load 40-60% for recovery

## Strength Training Principles

### Paper 20: Zatsiorsky, V. M., & Kraemer, W. J. (2006). *Science and Practice of Strength Training*. Human Kinetics.

**Key Findings:**
- Force-velocity relationship: heavier loads move slower
- Peak power at 30-70% 1RM for most athletes
- Training specificity: adapt to specific movements
- Exercise selection should mirror competition movements

**Practical Applications:**
- Include compound movements: squat, deadlift, bench, overhead press
- Train at velocities appropriate to goal
- Power training: lighter loads, explosive movement
- Exercise variety vs. specificity balance

### Paper 21: American College of Sports Medicine (ACSM). (2009). American College of Sports Medicine position stand. Progression models in resistance training for healthy adults. *Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise*, 41(3), 687.

**Key Findings:**
- Beginner: 1-3 sets, 60-70% 1RM, 2-3 days/week
- Intermediate: 3-6 sets, 60-80% 1RM, 3-4 days/week
- Advanced: 3-6 sets, 80-95% 1RM, 4-6 days/week
- Progression: increase resistance 2-10% when current load achievable

**Practical Applications:**
- Use ACSM guidelines for exercise prescription
- Adjust volume and intensity by experience
- Progress when target reps achieved with good form
- Regular deload every 4-8 weeks

### Paper 22: Garber, C. E., et al. (2011). American College of Sports Medicine position stand. Quantity and Quality of Exercise for Developing and Maintaining Cardiorespiratory, Musculoskeletal, and Neuromotor Fitness in Apparently Healthy Adults. *Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise*, 43(7), 1334.

**Key Findings:**
- Resistance training 2-3 days/week minimum
- 8-10 exercises per session major muscle groups
- 1 set sufficient for beginners, 2-3 for intermediate/advanced
- Progressive overload essential for continued adaptation

**Practical Applications:**
- Minimum effective dose: 2 strength sessions per week
- Full-body vs. split routines both effective
- Consistency more important than perfect program
- Recovery between sessions: 48 hours for muscle groups

## Warm-Up & Mobility

### Paper 23: Bishop, D. (2003). Warm Up II. *Sports Medicine*, 32(6), 261-279.

**Key Findings:**
- Dynamic warm-up superior to static stretching
- 5-10 min warm-up improves performance
- Includes light cardio and dynamic stretching
- Static stretching pre-workout reduces power output

**Practical Applications:**
- 5-10 min light cardio before training
- Dynamic stretches: leg swings, arm circles, torso twists
- Save static stretching for post-workout
- Sport-specific movements in warm-up

### Paper 24: Young, W. B., & Behm, D. G. (2002). Effects of Running, Static Stretching and Dynamic Stretching. *European Journal of Applied Physiology*, 86(3), 246.

**Key Findings:**
- Static stretching pre-workout reduces power output
- Dynamic stretching maintains or improves performance
- 5-10 min dynamic stretching optimal
- Static stretching post-workout beneficial

**Practical Applications:**
- Dynamic warm-up before every session
- Static stretching in cool-down
- Hold stretches 15-30 seconds
- Focus on muscles used in workout

## Injury Prevention

### Paper 25: van der Hoeven, H., et al. (2012). Injury Risk Reduction. *Sports Medicine*, 42(5), 397-408.

**Key Findings:**
- Eccentric training reduces injury risk
- Proper technique critical for injury prevention
- Workload management prevents overuse injuries
- Warm-up reduces injury risk by 50%

**Practical Applications:**
- Teach technique before loading
- Gradual progression of volume and intensity
- Include deload weeks regularly
- Never skip warm-up

## Concurrent Training

### Paper 26: Docherty, D., & Sporer, B. (2000). A Proposed Model for Concurrent Training. *Sports Medicine*, 30(5), 389-396.

**Key Findings:**
- Concurrent strength and endurance training causes interference effect
- Strength development impaired by concurrent endurance
- Endurance development less affected
- Recovery key to managing interference

**Practical Applications:**
- Separate sessions by 6+ hours when possible
- Prioritize running quality before heavy strength
- Consider block periodization: strength base, then running emphasis
- Monitor for signs of overtraining
